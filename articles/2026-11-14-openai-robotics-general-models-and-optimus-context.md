# OpenAI Folds Robotics Team Into Core Research as General Models Begin Controlling Robots

On November 13, 2026, OpenAI confirmed to Fortune that it has dissolved its standalone robotics team for the second time in six years. Robotics researchers have been reassigned to the company’s main research organization under Chief Research Officer Mark Chen, while applied robotics and hardware staff moved into the broader Applied organization led by President Greg Brockman. A company spokesperson stated that robotics remains a core part of OpenAI’s long-term vision and that the reorganization is intended to accelerate progress rather than retreat from the field.

The stated rationale is a shift in technical approach. Earlier in 2026, researchers had assumed that building specialized robotics models on top of video-generation systems such as Sora would be the most effective path. Subsequent results with general-purpose frontier models, including GPT-6 Astra, indicated that those models could be applied directly to robot control. In one reported Nvidia study on a simulated manipulation benchmark, an Astra-powered agent achieved a 74.8 percent success rate versus 51 percent for the best specialized robotics models.

## Historical Context

OpenAI previously shut down a robotics effort around 2020, citing limited training data. A new team was rebuilt in 2024 and partnered with the humanoid startup Figure; that collaboration ended in early 2025. The current reorganization is different in tone: the company is not exiting robotics but is betting that the same general models driving language, coding, and agent capabilities can also handle physical control without a dedicated intermediate stack.

The company still targets a first public demonstration of its own humanoid robot in 2027. The reorganization occurred on short notice; OpenAI had already booked a booth at the Conference on Robot Learning that subsequently sat empty.

## Broader Industry Context: Tesla Optimus and Other Humanoids

The OpenAI move coincides with continued activity elsewhere in humanoid robotics. Tesla has begun producing what it calls the first “production-quality” Optimus units on high-volume lines at its Fremont factory. Elon Musk has described the stage as early production and has said that reaching a rate of roughly 20,000 robots per week will take at least a year. External deliveries to companies outside Tesla are targeted for around the end of 2027, initially for industrial tasks. Challenges remain in hand design, fine motor control, reliability, and the AI needed for non-scripted real-world work.

Other developments include Agile Robots raising a $500 million Series F, GXO Logistics signing a letter of intent for up to 1,000 Apptronik Apollo humanoids, and various factory and service trials (including AgiBot systems in retail settings). Google DeepMind’s Gemini Robotics 2.5 has reported high success rates on live automotive production lines. These efforts continue to treat specialized robot models, simulation, and hardware integration as important, even as OpenAI explores a more general-model-centric approach.

## Implications of the General-Model Bet

If frontier models can reliably control robots with appropriate harnesses, perception tools, and contact-aware actions, the need for large dedicated robotics research teams may diminish. This could lower the barrier for companies that already have strong general models to enter physical AI. At the same time, real-world robotics still requires robust hardware, low-latency control loops, safety systems, and large amounts of physical interaction data—elements that general models alone do not automatically provide.

OpenAI’s decision to fold the team inward suggests the company believes the fastest path now runs through improvements to its core models rather than parallel specialized robotics research. Whether that bet pays off will be clearer once public demonstrations occur and independent evaluations measure performance on unstructured physical tasks rather than simulated benchmarks.

The reorganization also raises questions about talent allocation and research focus across the industry. As more labs experiment with applying general models to robots, the relative value of specialized versus general approaches will continue to be tested in both simulation and on real hardware.
